## 1. Depreciation (MACRS)

### How It Works
Depreciation allows rental property owners to recover the cost of the building (not land) over time through annual tax deductions. It is a non-cash deduction that reduces taxable rental income. Residential rental property is depreciated under the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS) using the General Depreciation System (GDS) or the Alternative Depreciation System (ADS).

### Key Rules for Residential Rental Property

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Recovery period (GDS)** | 27.5 years (straight-line) |
| **Recovery period (ADS)** | 30 years (straight-line, if elected or required) |
| **Convention** | Mid-month convention |
| **Depreciable basis** | Purchase price minus land value + improvements − land |
| **Annual rate (GDS)** | 3.636% of depreciable basis (Year 1 is prorated by month placed in service) |

### When Depreciation Begins and Ends
- **Begins:** When the property is "placed in service" — ready and available for rent
- **Ends:** The earlier of (a) full cost recovery, or (b) retirement from service

### What CAN Be Depreciated
- The building/structure itself
- Improvements (new roof, HVAC, additions)
- Appliances, carpeting, fencing (5, 7, or 15-year property — see cost segregation)
- Land improvements (15-year property under GDS)

### What CANNOT Be Depreciated
- Land (never depreciable)
- Property used for personal purposes
- Property placed in service and disposed of in the same year

### Eligibility
- Property must be owned by the taxpayer
- Property must be used in an income-producing activity (rental)
- Property must have a determinable useful life exceeding one year

### Dollar Limits
- No annual dollar cap on depreciation (unlike Section 179)
- The deduction is based solely on the depreciable basis and recovery period

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 4562** — Depreciation and Amortization (Part III for MACRS)
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — Supplemental Income and Loss (line 18)

### Key Deadlines
- File with annual tax return (April 15, or October 15 with extension)
- Depreciation must be claimed in the year it is allowable; you cannot skip years

## 2. Bonus Depreciation & Section 179

### Bonus Depreciation (Special Depreciation Allowance)

#### How It Works
Bonus depreciation allows an immediate first-year deduction of a percentage of the cost of qualified property with a recovery period of 20 years or less (including certain building components identified through cost segregation, such as 5-, 7-, and 15-year property).

#### 2026 Status — CRITICAL
The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 established a phase-down schedule for 100% bonus depreciation:

| Tax Year | Original TCJA Rate |
|----------|-------------------|
| 2018–2022 | 100% |
| 2023 | 80% |
| 2024 | 60% |
| 2025 | 40% |
| **2026** | **20%** |
| 2027+ | 0% |

**However**, legislation enacted in 2025 restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property acquired and placed into service after January 19, 2025. As of September 2026, the status of 100% bonus depreciation for tax year 2026 depends on whether Congress has enacted a further extension. **Check current law before filing.** If no extension was enacted, bonus depreciation is **20% for 2026** under the original TCJA phase-down.

#### Eligibility
- Property must be new or used (acquired from unrelated party)
- Recovery period of 20 years or less
- Qualified improvement property (QIP) — interior improvements to nonresidential buildings — eligible for 15-year recovery and bonus
- **Does NOT apply** to the building structure itself (27.5-year property) unless via cost segregation

#### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 4562** — Part II, Line 14 (Special Depreciation Allowance)

### Section 179 Expensing

#### How It Works
Section 179 allows immediate expensing of qualified property (equipment, furniture, appliances) up to an annual limit. Traditionally, Section 179 was NOT available for residential rental property buildings, but is available for personal property used in rental activities (appliances, furniture, carpeting in furnished rentals).

#### Dollar Limits (2025 figures; 2026 TBD)
- **Maximum deduction:** $2,500,000 (2025)
- **Phase-out threshold:** $4,000,000 of qualified property placed in service
- During phase-out, deduction is reduced dollar-for-dollar for costs exceeding $4,000,000

#### Eligibility
- Tangible personal property used in the rental activity
- Property must be placed in service during the tax year
- Deduction cannot exceed taxable income from the business

## 3. Cost Segregation

### How It Works
Cost segregation is an engineering-based study that reclassifies components of a building into shorter recovery periods for accelerated depreciation. Instead of depreciating the entire building over 27.5 years, specific components are reclassified:

| Component Category | Recovery Period (GDS) |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| Land improvements (parking, landscaping, fencing) | 15 years |
| Personal property (appliances, carpeting, furniture) | 5 or 7 years |
| Qualified Improvement Property (QIP) | 15 years |
| Building structure (remainder) | 27.5 years |

### Key Benefits
- **Front-loaded deductions:** Shorter recovery periods mean larger annual depreciation deductions
- **Bonus depreciation eligibility:** 5-, 7-, and 15-year property qualifies for bonus depreciation
- **Catch-up depreciation:** When performed on a property owned for years, prior "missed" depreciation can be claimed via Form 3115 (Change in Accounting Method) without amending prior returns

### Eligibility
- Any residential rental property placed in service
- Most cost-effective for properties with depreciable basis > $500,000
- Requires a professional cost segregation study by qualified engineers/CPAs

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 4562** — Depreciation and Amortization
- **Form 3115** — Application for Change in Accounting Method (for catch-up on prior years)

### Dollar Impact
- Varies significantly by property; typical studies reclassify 10-30% of building cost to shorter-life assets
- On a $500,000 building, reclassifying 20% ($100,000) to 5-year and 15-year property can generate $100,000 in bonus depreciation plus ongoing accelerated deductions

### Source URLs
- [IRS Audit Techniques Guide — Cost Segregation](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/cost-segregation-audit-techniques-guide)
- [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)

## 4. Mortgage Interest Deduction

### How It Works
Rental property owners can deduct **100% of mortgage interest** paid on loans used to purchase, build, or improve rental property. Unlike the home mortgage interest deduction for primary residences, there is **no dollar cap** on the amount of mortgage debt for rental properties.

### Key Points
- Interest on loans secured by the rental property is fully deductible
- Interest on home equity loans or lines of credit used for rental property improvements is deductible
- Points paid to obtain a mortgage must be amortized over the loan term (not fully deductible in year paid)
- Mortgage interest is reported on **Form 1098** from the lender
- If you pay $600+ in mortgage interest to any one lender, you should receive Form 1098
- **Refinancing rules:** When you refinance for more than the previous outstanding balance, interest on the excess proceeds may not be fully deductible as a rental expense unless used for rental purposes

### Business Interest Expense Limitation (Section 163(j))
For tax years beginning in 2025 and beyond, the business interest expense limitation under Section 163(j) may apply. For 2025, the calculation of adjusted taxable income includes add-backs for depreciation, amortization, and depletion. This generally means most rental property owners (with gross receipts under $30 million, indexed) are exempt, but large portfolios should verify.

### Eligibility
- Loan must be secured by the rental property or proceeds used for rental property
- Interest must be paid or accrued during the tax year

### Dollar Limits
- **No cap** on deductible mortgage interest for rental properties

### IRS Form(s)
- Reported on **Schedule E (Form 1040)**, Line 12 (Mortgage interest paid to banks, etc.)
- **Form 8990** — Limitation on Business Interest Expense (if Section 163(j) applies)

### Source URLs
- [IRS Publication 527 — Interest Expense](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)
- [IRS Topic No. 505 — Interest Expense](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505)

## 5. Operating Expense Deductions

### How It Works
All "ordinary and necessary" expenses of operating a rental property are fully deductible against rental income. These are reported on Schedule E (Form 1040).

### Comprehensive List of Deductible Operating Expenses

| Expense Category | Examples | Notes |
|-----------------|----------|-------|
| **Advertising** | Online listings, signage, print ads | Fully deductible |
| **Auto & Travel** | Mileage, airfare, hotels for property visits | 2025 standard mileage rate: 70¢/mile (2026 rate TBD) |
| **Cleaning & Maintenance** | Janitorial, pool service, landscaping | Fully deductible unless improvement |
| **Commissions** | Leasing agent fees, referral fees | Fully deductible |
| **Insurance** | Landlord/fire/liability/flood insurance | Premiums paid in advance must be prorated |
| **Legal & Professional Fees** | Attorney fees, CPA/tax preparation, eviction costs | Tax prep fees for Schedule E are deductible |
| **Management Fees** | Property management company | Fully deductible |
| **Repairs** | Fixing leaks, painting, appliance repair | Must be repairs, not improvements (see Section 14) |
| **Supplies** | Light bulbs, filters, cleaning supplies | Fully deductible |
| **Utilities** | Water, sewer, gas, electric, trash | Deductible if paid by landlord |
| **HOA/Condo Fees** | Homeowner association dues | Fully deductible |
| **Licenses & Permits** | Rental licenses, inspection fees | Fully deductible |
| **Office Expenses** | Postage, stationery, software | Fully deductible |
| **Equipment Rental** | Tools, machinery for maintenance | Fully deductible |
| **Education** | Seminars, books, subscriptions related to rental | Must relate to existing rental activity |

### Transportation Rules
- **Local transportation:** Deductible for collecting rent, managing, or maintaining property
- **Commuting:** Generally NOT deductible unless you have a qualifying home office as principal place of business
- **Methods:** Actual expenses OR standard mileage rate (70¢/mile for 2025; 2026 TBD)
- **Recordkeeping required:** Log of date, miles, and business purpose for each trip

### Travel Away From Home
- Deductible if the **primary purpose** is rental activity (collecting rent, managing, maintaining)
- Must allocate expenses if trip is both personal and business
- 50% limit on meal deductions

### Pre-Rental Expenses
- Deductible ordinary and necessary expenses from the time property is **made available** for rent (not necessarily when first rented)

### Eligibility
- Expenses must be **ordinary** (common in the industry) and **necessary** (helpful for the business)
- Must be paid or incurred during the tax year
- For properties with personal use, expenses must be prorated

### Dollar Limits
- No dollar cap on operating expense deductions
- Auto expenses: standard mileage rate or actual expenses

### IRS Form(s)
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — Part I (lines 5–19)

### Source URLs
- [IRS Publication 527 — Rental Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)
- [IRS Publication 463 — Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463)

## 6. Property Tax Deductions

### How It Works
State and local real property taxes paid on rental property are fully deductible as a rental expense on **Schedule E**. This is separate from the Schedule A itemized deduction for personal residence property taxes and is **not subject to the SALT deduction cap** when claimed as a business expense.

### Key Points
- **Rental property taxes → Schedule E:** Fully deductible, no SALT cap applies
- **Personal residence property taxes → Schedule A:** Subject to the SALT deduction limit
- Local benefit assessments (sidewalks, streets, sewers) are generally **not deductible** as taxes — they must be capitalized and depreciated
- However, local benefit taxes for **maintenance, repairs, or interest** charges are deductible
- Occupancy taxes, hospitality taxes on short-term rentals are also deductible

### SALT Deduction Context (2025 rules; 2026 TBD)
For **personal** (non-rental) property taxes claimed on Schedule A, the 2025 SALT deduction limit is $40,000 ($20,000 MFS), phased down for MAGI over $500,000 ($250,000 MFS), not below $10,000 ($5,000 MFS). This was a TCJA-era provision. **For rental properties on Schedule E, the SALT cap does not apply** — property taxes are a fully deductible business expense.

### Eligibility
- Tax must be levied uniformly against all real property in the jurisdiction
- Tax must be based on assessed value
- Must be paid during the tax year (cash basis)

### Dollar Limits
- **No cap** for rental property taxes claimed on Schedule E
- SALT cap applies only to personal/itemized deductions on Schedule A

### IRS Form(s)
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — Line 16 (Taxes)

### Source URLs
- [IRS Publication 527 — Taxes](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)
- [IRS Topic No. 503 — Deductible Taxes](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc503)

## 7. Section 199A — QBI Pass-Through Deduction

### ⚠️ CRITICAL 2026 STATUS: Potential Sunset

**The Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction was enacted as part of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 and, by statute, applies to tax years beginning after December 31, 2017, and ending on or before December 31, 2025.** As of the IRS's May 12, 2026 update, the agency's official page still lists this sunset date. **Unless Congress has extended Section 199A beyond 2025, this deduction is NOT available for tax year 2026.**

If legislation has been enacted since May 2026 to extend QBI, the information below applies. Otherwise, skip this section for 2026 tax planning.

### How It Works (If Extended)
The Section 199A deduction allows eligible taxpayers to deduct up to **20% of Qualified Business Income (QBI)** from a domestic business operated as a sole proprietorship, partnership, S corporation, trust, or estate — including certain rental real estate activities.

### Calculation
The deduction is the **lesser of:**
1. **QBI Component** (20% of QBI) + **REIT/PTP Component** (20% of qualified REIT dividends + qualified PTP income)
2. **20% of (taxable income − net capital gain)**

### Limitations (for higher-income taxpayers)
For taxpayers above certain taxable income thresholds:
- **Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB) exclusion:** Certain service businesses phase out
- **W-2 wage limitation:** deduction limited to 50% of W-2 wages paid by the business
- **UBIA limitation:** deduction limited to 25% of W-2 wages + 2.5% of unadjusted basis of qualified property

### Rental Real Estate Safe Harbor (Rev. Proc. 2019-38 / Notice 2019-07)
To qualify rental real estate for the QBI deduction, the IRS provides a safe harbor. Under this safe harbor, a **rental real estate enterprise** is treated as a trade or business for Section 199A if ALL of the following are met:

1. **Separate books and records** are maintained for each rental enterprise
2. **250+ hours** of rental services are performed per year (by owners, employees, agents, or independent contractors)
3. **Contemporaneous records** (time reports, logs) are maintained for the 250 hours
4. The taxpayer attaches a **signed statement** to the tax return annually

**Note:** A rental real estate enterprise that does not meet the safe harbor may still qualify if it rises to the level of a Section 162 trade or business under general principles. The safe harbor is optional, not mandatory.

Rental services include: advertising, lease negotiation, tenant screening, rent collection, maintenance/repairs, property management, supervision of employees/contractors, and daily operation.

### Eligibility
- Individual, trust, or estate taxpayer
- Rental activity constitutes a trade or business (or meets safe harbor)
- Income is from a U.S. source

### 2025 Income Thresholds (2026 thresholds TBD)
- **Married filing jointly:** $383,900 (2025 figure)
- **All other filers:** $191,950 (2025 figure)
- Above these thresholds, W-2 wage and UBIA limitations begin to apply

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 8995** — Qualified Business Income Deduction Simplified Computation (income ≤ threshold)
- **Form 8995-A** — Qualified Business Income Deduction (income > threshold or multiple businesses)
- Attach signed safe harbor statement if relying on Rev. Proc. 2019-38

### Source URLs
- [IRS — Qualified Business Income Deduction](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/qualified-business-income-deduction) *(Updated May 12, 2026)*
- [IRS Notice 2019-07 — Rental Real Estate Safe Harbor](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-19-07.pdf)

## 8. 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges

### How It Works
Under Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code, a rental property owner can sell investment property and defer all capital gains taxes by reinvesting the proceeds into "like-kind" replacement property. This is a **tax deferral**, not a tax elimination — the deferred gain is carried forward into the replacement property's basis.

### Key Rules (The "4 Rules")

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **1. Qualified Purpose** | Both relinquished and replacement property must be held for investment or business use (NOT personal use, NOT fix-and-flip inventory) |
| **2. Same Taxpayer** | The same legal entity/person that sells must buy (cannot sell as individual, buy through LLC) |
| **3. 45-Day Identification** | Within 45 days of sale closing, identify potential replacement properties in writing |
| **4. 180-Day Closing** | Close on replacement property within 180 days of sale (or tax return due date, if earlier) |

### Identification Rules
- **3-Property Rule:** Identify up to 3 properties of any value
- **200% Rule:** Identify any number of properties as long as aggregate FMV ≤ 200% of relinquished property's value
- **95% Rule:** If identifying more than the above, must acquire 95% of identified properties' aggregate value

### Boot
- "Boot" is any non-like-kind property received (cash, debt relief, personal property)
- Boot is taxable in the year of exchange
- To fully defer: replacement property value must ≥ relinquished property value, and all equity must be reinvested

### 2026 Limitation
The TCJA limited Section 1031 to **real property only** (not personal property) effective for exchanges after December 31, 2017. This limitation is **permanent** and continues through 2026 and beyond.

### Eligibility
- Both properties must be held for productive use in trade/business or investment
- Must be real property (as of 2018+)
- Primary residences and fix-and-flip properties do NOT qualify (but see Section 121 exclusion for personal residences)

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 8824** — Like-Kind Exchanges
- **Form 4562** — Depreciation for replacement property
- **Schedule D (Form 1040)** — If boot/gain is recognized

### Key Deadlines
- **45 calendar days** from closing to identify replacement(s) — **NO extensions, NO exceptions**
- **180 calendar days** (or tax return due date including extensions, if earlier) to close — **NO extensions**

### Source URLs
- [IRS — Like-Kind Exchanges](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips)
- [IRS Form 8824 Instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824)

## 9. Home Office Deduction

### How It Works
Rental property owners who use a portion of their home exclusively and regularly for managing their rental business may deduct a portion of home expenses. This converts otherwise non-deductible personal expenses into business deductions.

### Two Methods

#### Regular Method
- Calculate the percentage of home used for business (sq ft of office ÷ total home sq ft)
- Apply that percentage to: mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities, insurance, repairs, depreciation, etc.
- **Direct expenses** (painting the office): 100% deductible
- **Indirect expenses** (utilities, insurance): pro-rated by percentage

#### Simplified Method (Rev. Proc. 2013-13)
- **$5 per square foot** of qualified business use
- **Maximum 300 square feet** = maximum $1,500 deduction per year
- No depreciation component (simpler, no recapture on home sale)
- Cannot create or increase a net operating loss

### Eligibility Requirements (ALL must be met)
1. **Exclusive use:** The space must be used ONLY for business (no guest bedroom/office combo)
2. **Regular use:** Used on a continuing basis (not occasional)
3. **One of the following:**
   - **Principal place of business** for the rental activity
   - Place to meet with tenants, clients, or customers
   - Separate structure not attached to the home
   - **Administrative/management activities** performed there and no other fixed location for these activities

### Key "Administrative Safe Harbor"
Even if you do maintenance at properties, your home office can qualify as the principal place of business if you handle administrative tasks (bookkeeping, tenant calls, scheduling repairs, advertising) from the home and have **no other fixed location** for these activities.

### Dollar Limits
- Gross income limitation: home office deduction cannot exceed gross rental income minus non-home-office expenses
- Excess can be carried forward to future years
- Simplified method maximum: $1,500/year

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 8829** — Expenses for Business Use of Your Home
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — for rental-related home office (or Schedule C if reporting as a business)

### Source URLs
- [IRS Topic No. 509 — Business Use of Home](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc509)
- [IRS Publication 587 — Business Use of Your Home](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p587)

## 10. Passive Activity Loss Rules

### How It Works
Rental real estate activities are **per se passive** under the tax code, regardless of the owner's level of involvement. This means rental losses generally can only offset **passive income**, not ordinary income (W-2 wages, business income, investment income).

### The $25,000 Special Allowance (Active Participation Exception)

Rental property owners who **actively participate** can deduct up to **$25,000** of rental real estate losses against non-passive income annually.

#### Active Participation Defined
- Less stringent than "material participation"
- You must own at least 10% of the rental activity
- You must make management decisions (approving tenants, setting rent terms, approving expenditures)
- **You do NOT need to perform the work yourself** — using a property manager does not disqualify you
- **Limited partners DO NOT qualify** for active participation

#### MAGI Phaseout
The $25,000 special allowance phases out at a rate of **50 cents per dollar** for Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) above $100,000:

| MAGI | Maximum Allowance |
|------|-------------------|
| ≤ $100,000 | $25,000 |
| $125,000 | $12,500 |
| ≥ $150,000 | $0 |

**IMPORTANT:** These MAGI thresholds ($100,000–$150,000) are **NOT inflation-adjusted** — they have been fixed by statute since 1994.

#### Married Filing Separately
- If lived apart all year: $12,500 max, phased out at $50,000–$75,000 MAGI
- If lived together at any time: $0 allowance

### Carrying Forward Suspended Losses
- Disallowed passive losses are **carried forward indefinitely**
- Fully deductible in year of **complete disposition** of the activity (sale)
- Can offset passive income from other sources in future years

### At-Risk Rules (Additional Layer)
- Losses limited to amount "at risk" in the activity
- Typically includes cash invested + recourse debt
- Non-recourse debt generally qualifies for real estate
- **Form 6198** — At-Risk Limitations

### Excess Business Loss Limitation (Additional Layer)
For 2025, non-corporate taxpayers cannot deduct business losses exceeding:
- **$313,000** (single) / **$626,000** (married filing jointly) (2025; 2026 TBD, indexed)
- Disallowed loss treated as NOL carryforward
- **Form 461** — Excess Business Loss Limitation

### Eligibility
- Active participation (not material participation) for $25,000 allowance
- 10%+ ownership interest
- MAGI under $150,000 for full/partial allowance

### Dollar Limits (Statutory — NOT Inflation-Adjusted)
| Threshold | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Maximum special allowance | $25,000 ($12,500 MFS) |
| Phaseout begins | $100,000 MAGI |
| Phaseout ends | $150,000 MAGI |

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 8582** — Passive Activity Loss Limitations
- **Form 8582-CR** — Passive Activity Credit Limitations
- **Form 6198** — At-Risk Limitations
- **Form 461** — Excess Business Loss Limitation
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)**

### Source URLs
- [IRS Topic No. 425 — Passive Activities — Losses and Credits](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc425) *(Updated April 28, 2026)*
- [IRS Publication 925 — Passive Activity and At-Risk Rules](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)

## 11. Real Estate Professional Status

### How It Works
Taxpayers who qualify as **real estate professionals** (REPS) can treat rental activities as **non-passive**, meaning rental losses can offset ordinary income without the $25,000 cap or MAGI phaseout. This is one of the most powerful tax benefits available.

### Eligibility Requirements (ALL Must Be Met)

#### 1. The 750-Hour Test
- More than **750 hours** of personal services performed in real property trades or businesses during the year
- Real property trades or businesses include: development, redevelopment, construction, reconstruction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, or brokerage

#### 2. The 50% Test
- More than **50%** of all personal services performed during the year are in real property trades or businesses
- If you have a W-2 job at 2,000 hours/year, you would need 2,000+ hours in real estate to qualify — extremely difficult

#### 3. Material Participation
- In addition to qualifying as a REPS, you must **materially participate** in EACH rental activity (or elect to aggregate all rental interests as one activity)

#### Material Participation Tests (Any One of 7):
1. 500+ hours in the activity
2. Substantially all participation is by the taxpayer
3. 100+ hours and at least as much as anyone else
4. Significant participation activities (100-500 hours each) with aggregate of 500+ hours
5. Material participation in any 5 of the prior 10 years
6. Personal service activity with material participation in any 3 prior years
7. Facts and circumstances showing regular, continuous, substantial involvement

### Aggregation Election
- A qualifying REPS can elect to treat **all** rental real estate interests as **one activity**
- This makes it easier to meet the material participation threshold across multiple properties
- Election is binding for future years unless revoked with IRS consent
- Must attach a **formal election statement** to the tax return

### Documentation Requirements
- **Contemporaneous time logs** are essential
- IRS regularly challenges REPS status and requires proof
- Records should include: date, time spent, description of service, property address
- Post-facto reconstructions are not reliable for audit defense

### Spousal Rules
- Spouses file jointly: only the qualifying spouse's hours count toward the 750-hour test
- The non-qualifying spouse does not benefit from REPS status for their ownership share
- However, if one spouse qualifies and material participation is met, the joint return benefits

### Dollar Impact
- **Unlimited** rental losses can offset ordinary income (no $25,000 cap)
- Can be worth tens of thousands in tax savings for high-income earners with significant rental losses (primarily from depreciation)

### IRS Form(s)
- No separate REPS election form
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — check box for real estate professional
- **Form 8582** — still filed but losses flow through as non-passive if qualified
- Elections attached as statements to the return

### Source URLs
- [IRS Publication 925 — Real Estate Professional Rules](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)

## 12. Casualty and Theft Losses

### How It Works
If rental property is damaged or destroyed by a sudden, unexpected event (fire, storm, vandalism, theft), the owner may deduct the loss. For **business/rental property**, the rules are more favorable than for personal-use property.

### Calculation
- **Loss amount:** Lesser of (a) adjusted basis or (b) decrease in FMV
- **Reduced by:** Any insurance reimbursement or other compensation
- **For complete destruction:** Loss = adjusted basis minus insurance/salvage
- **For partial damage:** Loss = decrease in FMV or cost of repairs (if actually repaired)

### Key Rules for Rental Property
- Unlike personal casualty losses (which are now only deductible in federally declared disaster areas under TCJA), **business/rental casualty losses remain fully deductible**
- $100 per-casualty floor and 10% AGI threshold do **NOT** apply to business/rental property
- Must be **sudden, unexpected, or unusual** — gradual deterioration is NOT deductible
- Theft losses require proof of theft (police report)

### Gain Situations
If insurance proceeds exceed adjusted basis, you have a **gain**. You may defer this gain by purchasing replacement property within the replacement period (generally 2 years, extended to 4 years for federally declared disaster areas).

### Eligibility
- Property must be used in a rental/business activity
- Loss must be from an identifiable event that is sudden, unexpected, or unusual
- Loss must be evidenced by closed and completed transaction

### Dollar Limits
- No $100 floor per casualty (business property)
- No 10% AGI threshold (business property)
- Deduction limited to adjusted basis minus insurance

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 4684** — Casualties and Thefts (Section B for business property)
- **Schedule E (Form 1040)** — loss flows from Form 4684

### Source URLs
- [IRS Publication 547 — Casualties, Disasters, and Thefts](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547)
- [IRS Publication 527 — Casualties and Thefts section](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)

## 13. De Minimis Safe Harbor

### How It Works
Under the tangible property regulations (Treas. Reg. § 1.263(a)-1(f)), taxpayers can elect to **immediately deduct** (expense) purchases of tangible property up to a certain dollar threshold, rather than capitalizing and depreciating them. This is an **annual election** (not per-item).

### Dollar Thresholds
- **$2,500 per invoice or item** — for taxpayers with an applicable financial statement (AFS — audited financials)
- **$2,500 per invoice or item** — IRS simplified procedure for taxpayers WITHOUT an AFS
- Historically was $500 per item before the IRS simplified procedure

### Requirements
1. Must have a **written accounting policy** in place at the beginning of the tax year
2. Policy must state the dollar threshold for expensing
3. Policy must be applied consistently
4. Election is made annually by filing the tax return (no separate form)

### What It Covers
- Tangible personal property (tools, equipment, appliances, furniture)
- Materials and supplies
- Building improvements valued at or under the threshold
- **Does NOT apply to** inventory or land

### Practical Impact for Rental Owners
- A $2,400 refrigerator can be expensed immediately rather than depreciated over 5 years
- A $2,000 repair/replacement can be expensed immediately
- Simplifies recordkeeping and accelerates deductions

### Eligibility
- Any taxpayer with a rental activity
- Must have written accounting policy
- Must not be an inventory item

### IRS Form(s)
- No separate form — election is made by filing the return and applying the policy
- **Schedule E (Form 10440)** — expense is included in repairs or supplies

### Source URLs
- [IRS Tangible Property Regulations — De Minimis Safe Harbor](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tangible-property-final-regulations)
- [IRS Publication 527 — De Minimis Safe Harbor](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)

## 14. Small Taxpayer Safe Harbor for Repairs

### How It Works
Under Treas. Reg. § 1.263(a)-3(h), qualifying small taxpayers can elect to treat building improvements as **deductible repairs** (expensed immediately), even when those costs would normally need to be capitalized. This safe harbor applies to **buildings** (including residential rental property).

### Eligibility Requirements (ALL must be met)

1. **Gross receipts test:** Average annual gross receipts ≤ **$10 million** (indexed for inflation; 2025 amount) for the three prior tax years
2. **Building basis test:** Total amount paid for repairs, maintenance, improvements, and similar activities during the year does NOT exceed the **lesser of:**
   - **$10,000**, OR
   - **2% of the unadjusted basis of the building**
3. The building has an unadjusted basis of $1,000,000 or less
4. Election is made annually on the tax return

### What It Covers
- Repairs and maintenance
- Improvements to the building structure or systems
- Costs that would normally be capitalized as betterments, restorations, or adaptations

### Practical Example
- Building basis: $500,000
- 2% of basis: $10,000
- Annual repair/improvement limit: the lesser of $10,000 or $10,000 = $10,000
- If total repairs/improvements for the year = $9,000, ALL can be expensed
- If total = $12,000, the safe harbor does NOT apply; each cost must be analyzed

### Important Note
This safe harbor is **separate from** the de minimis safe harbor (Section 13 above) and the safe harbor for routine maintenance. A taxpayer can use different safe harbors for different costs.

### IRS Form(s)
- No separate form — election made by filing Schedule E
- Should retain documentation supporting eligibility

### Source URLs
- [IRS Tangible Property Regulations](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tangible-property-final-regulations)
- [IRS Publication 527 — Repairs and Improvements](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)

## 16. Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

### How It Works
The Net Investment Income Tax is a **3.8% surtax** on the lesser of (a) net investment income, or (b) the excess of Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) over the applicable threshold. Rental income is generally considered net investment income.

### Thresholds (Not Indexed for Inflation)
| Filing Status | MAGI Threshold |
|---------------|----------------|
| Married filing jointly | $250,000 |
| Married filing separately | $125,000 |
| Single / Head of household | $200,000 |
| Qualifying widow(er) | $250,000 |

### What Is Included in Net Investment Income
- Rental income (unless from a trade or business in which you materially participate as a REPS)
- Interest, dividends, capital gains
- Annuity income
- Royalty income
- Passive activity income

### Real Estate Professional Exception
If you qualify as a real estate professional (Section 11) and materially participate in the rental activity, the rental income is **not** considered net investment income and is **not subject** to the 3.8% NIIT.

### IRS Form(s)
- **Form 8960** — Net Investment Income Tax — Individuals, Estates, and Trusts
- Filed with Form 1040

### Source URLs
- [IRS — Net Investment Income Tax](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax)
- [IRS Form 8960 Instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8960)
